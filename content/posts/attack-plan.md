A designated attack plan for learning Chu Nom I intend on going through

Hey there. I am [Chatterine Snuggles](https://linktr.ee/chatterine), one of the people behind this blog. I took the decision to learn demotic Vietnamese writing, better known as Chu Nom (𡨸喃, literally "Southern characters"). [To quote the Nôm Foundation:](https://nomfoundation.org/nom-script/What-is-Nom-?uiLang=en)

> Chữ Nôm, is the ancient _ideographic vernacular script_ of the Vietnamese language. After Vietnamese independence from China in 939 CE, chữ Nôm, an ideographic script that represents Vietnamese speech, became the national script.

Awesome, isn't it? Personally I first heard about chu Nom when I was doing some research on the Vietnamese language. After watching [Langfocus's overview on Vietnamese](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vQNud-Ra2Gw), I immediately become hooked on the idea of learning it. I've always been fascinated by Eastern languages cultures, from those times I went to a sushi bar[^1] to have some *takoyaki* with my elder brother when I was a kid, or when I played Koei's strategy games focused on things like the [Three Kingdoms novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_of_the_Three_Kingdoms).

## Why Nôm?

As for "why Vietnamese?", I don't really know, to be quite honest, haha. I guess I really do like obscure stuff[^2]! The sounds of the language are pleasant to my ears, too, especially those of the Saigonese dialect[^3].

This still doesn't explain why I chose to learn demotic writing system[^4], though. Being able to read and write in a dead language[^5] sounds *cool*, not because I want to flex, but because it means being able to trace a people back to their roots. Not to mention, hobbyists still learn other things that are deemed as "useless", such as Sumerian or Sanskrit. [Q.E.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q.E.D.)

Modern Vietnamese can easily be represented through a combination of Chinese characters and demotic, in a system called "chữ Hán Nôm." Thus, one could argue that Vietnamese vocabulary, spoken Vietnamese and chu Han Nom are closely intertwined. I am specifically curious about how Vietnamese was represented before *chữ Quốc ngữ* (the Vietnamese Latin script) took over.

Moreover, I'm personally interested in becoming a scholar who studies East Asian languages[^6]. I got in touch with a guy (let's call him "Jones", so as to protect his identity) who's trying to translate [The Tale of Kieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Kieu) into my native language. Personally I'd like to help Jones, or at the very least, become someone like him.

Even if in the end I don't really end up able to aid Jones with his project, I feel like demotic might be a useful skill to learn, at least regarding my hobbies.[^7] I have this goal of wanting to someday learn all four CJKV (Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese) languages to a conversationally fluent level.

## So, how are you going about it?

I've separated the strategy into a few unorganized talking points:

1. Using Anki and spaced repetition as a means to an end;
2. Learning the Nôm alongside the chữ Quốc ngữ forms of words;
3. Dabbling with learning Nom despite having an overall low Vietnamese ability;
4. Reading online books written in this script to do the "immersion."
5. Skipping learning Chinese characters or any form of Chinese altogether, and just going straight to the grind;

I'll elaborate further on those topics below.

I should also add that these are only what I *intend* on doing. As such, don't take these as a personal recommendation or anything. Without further ado, let's go on the explanations:

### Learning chữ Quốc ngữ alongside Nom

In a similar fashion to how Chinese learners don't learn the pronunciation of words in an isolated manner from Chinese *hanzi*, I believe this to be the most logical approach. The same could be said about Japanese kanji.

One possible counter-argument to this method would be that Korean learners and speakers learn to read Korean with its own native alphabet before ever tacking Korean written in Chinese *hanja*, assuming they ever decide to do so. But I only feel like this happens because hanja were fully phased out from Korean society, and most of the writing encountered in South Korean day-to-day life isn't available in Hanja as a result. This also links to the next topic, being...

I must preface this by saying that I'm not a total beginner to Vietnamese. Using English as a comparison, I am what some English as a Second Language teachers would call a [false beginner](https://www.theteflacademy.com/blog/what-are-absolute-beginner-and-false-beginner-efl-students/). This means that I've already started studying, but did not progress very far. I also have some knowledge of Chinese, a language of which I am also  a false beginner in.

Much like how some Japanese learners (especially those learning through an "immersion" method) learn first the kana and gain cursory knowledge of Kanji before attempting to dive deep into reading, and only after they get good at reading comics and novels they start listening, I precisely want to mimic this with Vietnamese, learning to read before listening.

Simply put, an "immersion approach" to language learning means learning like a native  would do, which is through input that is comprehensible. One example of an immersion-based approach is [Refold](https://refold.la/).

You'd be amazed at the amount of online materials and preserved resources that there are for Hán Nôm, despite it being a "dead" script.  These online resources include Anki decks.

I'm using a flashcard program called [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/), making me review electronic flashcards at intervals specifically calculated so as to maximize retention. Card collections are organized into "decks," which can be reviewed whenever and wherever you want.

Another kind of material are poems and media. There are growing communities on places such as Reddit and Facebook dedicated to the hobby, which share their creations. One could use those to learn.

Lastly, I briefly want to touch on preserved books. There's a number of ancient articles, essays and books written that are available for free in digital libraries. One day I intend on organizing a list of books to read.

This ties in with our topic, which is...

### Skipping learning Chinese

I'll demonstrate why this is a good idea with a personal anecdote of mine. Whenever I tell people I know in real life that I'm learning Vietnamese, they immediately get curious and start asking all sort of questions.

One such question is "what language does Vietnamese look like the most?", or in my own words, what language(s) *tiếng Việt* is related to. I have a canned response to this, being that Vietnamese contains loads of loanwords from Chinese called *từ Hán-Việt*, and that, due to coincidental similarities between the two, such as the fact both are isolating, tonal SVO language, one could argue that Vietnamese bears a passing resemblance to Chinese.

The two most asked follow-up questions are "ah, then Vietnamese must be a dialect of Chinese, much like how Mandarin and Cantonese are, right?" and "does this mean Vietnamese and Chinese speakers can understand each other?" I explain to my listener both are untrue, just like how [Spanish and Portuguese having Arabic borrowings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-3QML3tfBNQ) does not make them an Arabic language, and do not ensure mutual intelligibility.

Understand, my reader, the following: Vietnamese is _not_ a Chinese language. It's a _Sinified_ language. And because of this, one does not need to learn Chinese to learn modern-day Vietnamese, just like one doesn't need to learn Latin to learn English.

In identical fashion, one shouldn't need to learn Chinese to learn demotic Vietnamese. What one actually needs to learn is all [214 Kangxi radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangxi_radicals), since they are the building blocks of demotic.

Demotic chu Nom characters are built using the same "lego bricks" as *Hán tự* (漢字, "Chinese characters"). This means that all [six formations of Chinese "letters"](https://studycli.org/chinese-characters/types-of-chinese-characters/) also apply here. Feel free to skim the link, but the relevant part to this post is the "phono-semantic compounds."

Phono-semantic comes from the greek roots *phono-*, meaning "sound," and, *semanto*, which stands for "meaning." Combine both and you get "sound-meaning." This is relevant because several Nom are formed from a radical that gives us a clue to what the character means, and an additional element for indicating sound.

One example is the native Vietnamese word for "mountain," [𡶀](https://chunom.org/pages/21D80/) *núi*. The top element, [山](https://chunom.org/pages/?search=%E5%B1%B1), can be used as a standalone Chinese character that also means "mountain," whereas the bottom element, [内](https://chunom.org/pages/?search=%E5%86%85), is pronounced *nội* and marks pronunciation.
